# Week 11 Pitch: Narration Script

Used for the recorded pitch video (about 13 minutes at a measured pace).

## Slide 1: Title

Good morning, members of the Board. My name is Eugene Obunde Okumu, and I am presenting on behalf of Team Null Terminators.
Over the next fifteen minutes, we will show you how Kenya Pipeline Company can stop pump failures before they happen.
We will cover the problem, our solution, the evidence, the financial case, the risks, and the decision we are asking you to make today.

## Slide 2: The ask

Let me start with what we are asking for, so that you can listen to everything else with the decision in mind.
We ask the Board to approve a pilot at one station. The pilot costs five million Kenya shillings and runs for three months.
If it works, we roll the system out to all stations, for a further ten million Kenya shillings. At full scale, the system pays for itself in about seven months.
Notice the shape of this request. The Board is not being asked to commit fifteen million shillings today.
Five million is the most you put at risk, and the remaining ten million is released only if the pilot proves itself. I will come back to exactly how that works.

## Slide 3: The problem

Now, the problem.
KPC's pumps push fuel through one thousand three hundred and forty-two kilometres of pipeline. Today, those pumps are serviced on a fixed calendar, not according to their condition.
That creates two problems at once. Healthy pumps are serviced when they do not need it. And worn pumps can fail between scheduled visits.
When a pump fails, deliveries halt, costly emergency repairs are needed, and in the worst cases fuel leaks.
The stakes are not theoretical. KPC has lived through them.
In 2018, the Kiboko spill lost five hundred and fifty-one thousand litres of fuel. In product alone, that was worth about sixty-three million Kenya shillings.
To put a smaller number on it: a hairline leak that goes unnoticed for a single day loses roughly fourteen million shillings of fuel.
And in 2011, the Sinai fire began with a faulty part worth less than one thousand shillings. It cost KPC over one hundred and three million shillings directly, and about one hundred people died.
A calendar cannot see a part that is starting to fail. A sensor can.

## Slide 4: The solution

Here is what we propose.
We fit low-cost sensors to each pump. They track four things: vibration, temperature, pressure, and electrical load.
First, the system learns what a healthy pump looks like. It builds a normal profile for every pump.
Then, every day, it scores each pump in two ways. It rates the chance that the pump fails in the next seven days, and it estimates how many hours of life the pump has left.
Finally, it gives planners one ranked repair list, on a single screen, with email alerts.
The system also factors in the weather and local activity around each station, so that the scores reflect the conditions each station actually faces.

## Slide 5: The evidence

Does it work? We tested a prototype in Week Six, using simulated data for twenty-four pumps.
It caught eighty-four percent of failures early. And it raised false alarms at a rate of only two point four percent, which is about one in forty alerts.
In other words, imagine one hundred failures. The system flags eighty-four of them in time. And when it raises an alert, it is wrong roughly once in every forty.
I want to be direct about the limits of this evidence. These results come from simulated data, not from KPC's real pumps. Real-world accuracy could be lower.
That is not a footnote. It is the central reason we are proposing a pilot, rather than asking you to fund a full rollout on simulation alone.
Confirming these numbers on KPC's real pumps is the purpose of the pilot.

## Slide 6: What a planner gets

Let us look at what this means for the people who will use it.
Each day, a planner sees three things.
First, the chance that each pump fails in the next seven days, so attention goes where it is needed.
Second, the estimated hours of life remaining, so repairs can be planned in advance instead of being forced by an emergency.
Third, a single ranked repair list, on one screen, with email alerts.
We have also designed this for trust, because a system that planners ignore saves nothing. Every alert says, in plain words, why a pump is flagged and how many hours it has left.
And during the pilot, the system runs alongside the current schedule, so planners can compare its advice with what they would have done anyway.

## Slide 7: Financial impact

Now the numbers. I will show the calculation behind every figure, so that the Board can challenge it.
There are three sources of savings.
The first is fewer emergency repairs and delivery stoppages. We assume six failures a year. The system catches eighty-four percent of them early. Each failure caught early turns a four million shilling emergency repair into a one and a half million shilling planned repair, a saving of two and a half million. Six, times eighty-four percent, times two and a half million, gives twelve point six million shillings a year.
The second source is fuel loss avoided. If a Kiboko-size loss of sixty-three million shillings happens once every three years, that is twenty-one million a year. If the system prevents half of that, we save ten point five million a year.
The third source is less unnecessary servicing. If KPC does sixty services a year, and we skip a quarter of them, at four hundred thousand shillings each, that saves six million a year.
Add them up, and the total annual benefit is twenty-nine point one million shillings.
Running costs, covering cloud, sensor upkeep, and support, come to four million. So the net annual savings are twenty-five point one million shillings.
I should be transparent about one thing. The fleet size of thirty pumps, the failure count, and the repair costs are planning assumptions. We will replace them with KPC's actual figures early in the pilot.

## Slide 8: Investment and return

What does it cost, and what do you get back?
The one-off investment is fifteen million Kenya shillings: five million for the pilot, plus ten million for the rollout.
Of that fifteen million, thirty pumps at a quarter of a million shillings each for sensors comes to seven and a half million, and setup and training is another seven and a half million.
Divide fifteen million by the net annual savings of twenty-five point one million, and you get point six of a year. That is about seven months to pay back.
Over three years, the benefits are three times twenty-nine point one, which is eighty-seven point three million shillings.
The costs are the fifteen million investment, plus three years of running costs at four million each, which is twenty-seven million in total.
Benefits minus costs, divided by costs, gives a three-year return on investment of two hundred and twenty-three percent.

## Slide 9: Stress test

A good business case should survive pessimism, so we stress-tested it.
Suppose the benefits are only half of what I showed you. Payback stretches from seven months to about seventeen months. The three-year return falls from two hundred and twenty-three percent to sixty-two percent.
The chart shows both cases. Each line starts at minus fifteen million, which is the investment, and climbs as savings accumulate.
The base case crosses zero at around seven months, and ends just over sixty million shillings ahead after three years.
Even the half-benefit case crosses zero at around seventeen months, and ends about sixteen point seven million shillings ahead.
It is also worth saying what we did not count. Clean-up costs, legal claims, and lives protected are not in these figures, and each of them could exceed the numbers I have shown you.

## Slide 10: Risks

No plan is without risk, so let me name the two that matter most.
Risk one: the results are not yet proven on KPC's real pumps. Our eighty-four percent comes from simulated data, so real-world accuracy could be lower.
Our response is to test at one station first, so that only five million shillings is at stake. The remaining ten million is released only if the pilot catches at least eighty percent of failures, with no more than five percent false alarms.
Risk two: planners may not trust or act on the alerts, or data access may be slow.
Our response has three parts. Every alert explains, in plain words, why a pump is flagged and how many hours of life it has left. During the pilot, we run alongside the current schedule, so nobody has to bet their judgement on the system. And we agree a KPC engineering contact and data access before we start, so that no time is lost waiting for data.

## Slide 11: The plan and the gate

Here is how the plan unfolds.
Today, the Board approves five million shillings and three months, and KPC nominates an engineering contact who can give us access to historical pump failure records.
In months one to three, we run the pilot at one station, alongside the current schedule. Early on, we replace our planning assumptions with KPC's actual figures.
At the end of month three, we return to this Board with real results from KPC's own pumps.
Then comes the decision. The ten million shilling rollout goes ahead only if the pilot catches at least eighty percent of failures, with no more than five percent false alarms.
If it does not, the Board's total exposure is five million shillings, and we will have learned that at a fraction of the cost of a full rollout.

## Slide 12: Recommendation

Our recommendation is: go. Pilot first, then decide.
We ask the Board to do two things today.
Approve five million Kenya shillings and three months for a one-station pilot.
And nominate a KPC engineering contact with access to historical pump failure records.
At the end of the pilot, we will return with real results, and the ten million shilling fleet-wide rollout will go ahead only if the targets are met.
A pump that is watched can be repaired before it fails. This pilot lets us prove it.
